Abstract
We start by introducing a car insurance pricing problem which is the most typical example for applying regression modeling and machine learning tools. This example will serve us as a running example through many of the chapters and lectures.

1 Actuarial modeling – a pricing problem

  • We introduce a car insurance pricing problem.

  • This pricing problem is based on claims frequencies.

  • The analysis in this first lecture will mostly be empirical.

  • It considers the popular French motor third party liability (MTPL) claims dataset freMTPL2freq of Dutang, Charpentier and Gallic (2024).

  • Generally, raw data needs data cleaning, e.g., correcting for data errors, inputting missing values, merging partial information, etc.

  • The data presented here has already been cleaned; for the data cleaning applied see Appendix B in Wüthrich and Merz (2023).

1.2 Claims count modeling and frequencies

  • The above portfolio has \(n=678,007\) insurance policyholders. We label them by \(i \in \{1, \ldots, n\}\).

  • These policyholders are characterized by covariates \(\boldsymbol{X}_i\) describing their policy features such as the age of the car or the vehicle brand.

  • For each policyholder \(i\), there is an observed number of claims \(N_i \ge 0\) within the given calendar year.

  • The time exposures \(v_i \in (0, 1]\) give the proportions of the calendar year during which the policies have been active (exposed).

  • To account for the different time exposures, one typically studies and models the claims frequencies \[ Y_i = N_i/v_i. \]

  • The general task in actuarial pricing is to predict (forecast) the (future) claims frequencies \(Y_i\) as accurately as possible.

  • These predictions should take into account all available/relevant policyholder information \(\boldsymbol{X}_i\), as this information may reveal structural differences in their propensity to claims.

  • (Best) predictors are typically computed by conditional means \[ \mu(\boldsymbol{X}_i):=\mathbb{E}\left[Y_i \left| \boldsymbol{X}_i\right]\right. .\] This is the expected claim of a policyholder with covariates \(\boldsymbol{X}_i\).

  • Actuarial pricing one aims at finding an accurate regression function \[\boldsymbol{X} \mapsto \mu(\boldsymbol{X}),\] that reflects the expected claim as a function of the covariates \(\boldsymbol{X}\).

1.3 Regression modeling

  • In general, the true data generating mechanism/model is unknown.

  • Therefore, one cannot compute the regression function \(\boldsymbol{X} \mapsto \mu(\boldsymbol{X})\).

  • Thus, one needs to estimate \(\mu\) from past data \[ {\cal L} = \left(Y_i, \boldsymbol{X}_i, v_i\right)_{i=1}^n.\]

  • \({\cal L}\) is called learning sample because it is used to learn the unknown regression function \(\boldsymbol{X} \mapsto \mu(\boldsymbol{X})\).

  • Before solving this estimation/learning problem, one should always empirically study the available data \({\cal L} = (Y_i, \boldsymbol{X}_i, v_i)_{i=1}^n\).

  • This empirical study is crucial to fully understand the problem and to select a suitable class of candidate regression models \({\cal M}=\{\mu\}\).

2 Empirical data analysis

  • Before we try to find a suitable regression function \(\boldsymbol{X}\mapsto \mu(\boldsymbol{X})\), we empirically analyze the learning sample \({\cal L}\).

  • Such an empirical analysis of the learning sample \({\cal L}\) includes different sample statistics as well as visualizations of the data.

  • For simplicity, we only consider the two covariates DrivAge and Density in this preliminary analysis. Later, all covariates are considered.

  • We start by classifying the policies w.r.t. their numbers of claims.

library(tidyverse)

  • The big majority of insurance policies does not suffer any claims.

  • The empirical claims frequency is \(\sum_{i=1}^n N_i/\sum_{i=1}^n v_i=7.36\%\).

  • In machine learning, this is called a class imbalance problem.

  • In actuarial modeling, this is just the typical problem actuaries have to deal with, i.e., insurance claims are rare events.

  • Thus, actuaries try to find systematic structure in data in which claims are rare events.

  • (lhs): Class imbalance problem mentioned above.

  • (rhs): Distribution of exposures \((v_i)_{i=1}^n\), roughly 25% of the policies have a full-year exposure \(v_i=1\) in this data.

  • (lhs): Total exposure \(\sum_i v_i \mathbf{1}_{\{{\rm age_i}={\rm age}\}}\) per driver age (DrivAge).

  • (rhs): Empirical frequency \(\sum_i N_i \mathbf{1}_{\{{\rm age_i}={\rm age}\}}/\sum_i v_i \mathbf{1}_{\{{\rm age_i}={\rm age}\}}\) per DrivAge; confidence bounds correspond to 2 std in a Poisson model.

  • (lhs): Total exposure \(\sum_i v_i \mathbf{1}_{\{{\rm LD_i}={\rm LD}\}}\) per log-Density (class LD).

  • (rhs): Empirical frequency \(\sum_i N_i \mathbf{1}_{\{{\rm LD_i}={\rm LD}\}}/\sum_i v_i \mathbf{1}_{\{{\rm LD_i}={\rm LD}\}}\) per log-Density; confidence bounds correspond to 2 std in a Poisson model.

  • The previous plots show the marginal distributions of the claims.

  • Generally, covariates interact, and one cannot simply multiply marginal frequencies to receive the correct prices.

  • For example:

    • Young drivers may more likely live in the highly populated cities.
    • Thus, high frequencies at younger ages and in densely populated areas may be driven by the same risk factor.
    • Multiplying marginals will, thus, result in a double penalization of the same risk factor.

  • For this reason, we need to understand the multivariate structure to receive a good pricing functional \(\boldsymbol{X}\mapsto \mu(\boldsymbol{X})\).

  • In other words, we need to estimate \(\mu(\boldsymbol{X})\) jointly in the components of \(\boldsymbol{X}=(X_1,\ldots, X_q)^\top\), and not marginally.

  • The above heatmap shows the empirical frequency as a function of the two covariates DrivAge and log-Density.

  • The empirical data has been smoothed with the kernel smoothing method called loess (locally estimated scatter plot smoothing).

  • The colored dots show the loess results in the covariate combinations that exist in our portfolio, and the white space corresponds to covariate combinations that are not available in our portfolio.

  • The coloring shows quite some non-linear structure in the covariate components, and it does not support the pattern of multiplying marginals.

  • This requires regression modeling or machine learning solutions, e.g.,

    • generalized linear models (GLMs)
    • neural networks and deep learning
    • regression trees
    • gradient boosting machines (GBMs)

3 A generalized linear model example

  • We start with generalized linear models (GLMs) for the class \({\cal M}\) of the considered regression functionals \(\boldsymbol{X}\mapsto \mu(\boldsymbol{X})\).

  • The underlying mathematical theory is going to be presented in careful detail in the next lecture.

  • The most simple GLM setting considers \[ \boldsymbol{X}\mapsto \mu(\boldsymbol{X}) = \exp \left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1+ \beta_2 X_2\right),\] for \(\boldsymbol{X}=(X_1,X_2)=(\text{DrivAge}, \text{log-Density})\) and parameter \((\beta_0,\beta_1,\beta_2)\).

### Model GLM 1 (Poisson for counts with offset and canonical log-link)
d.glm1 <- glm(ClaimNb ~ DrivAge + lDensity, 
                        data=dat, offset=log(Exposure), family=poisson())
#
dat$GLM1 <- fitted(d.glm1)
summary(d.glm1)

### Model GLM 2
dat$DrivAgeGLM    <- as.factor(cut(dat$DrivAge, c(18,20,25,30,40,50,70,101), labels = c("18-20", "21-25", "26-30", "31-40", "41-50", "51-70", "71+"), include.lowest = TRUE))
#
# Poisson GLM with offset and (canonical) log-link
d.glm2 <- glm(ClaimNb ~ DrivAgeGLM + lDensity, 
                        data=dat, offset=log(Exposure), family=poisson())
#
dat$GLM2 <- fitted(d.glm2)

4 Gradient boosting machine

  • As a first machine learning regression model, we present a gradient boosting machine (GBM).

  • GBMs belong to the most powerful techniques on tabular data, in particular, the powerful versions like XGBoost, LightGBM or CatBoost.

  • A GBM partitions the covariate space into different (homogeneous) subsets and it estimates the frequency on each subset.

  • We only present the resulting heatmap on the two selected covariates; for technical details we refer to our ‘AI Tools for Actuaries’ homepage.

  • GBMs are not further studied in this summer school.

  • This result was generated by the LightGBM version.

  • We interpret this as follows.

  • The GBM seems to reflect the observed (smoothed) data very well.

  • The only criticism that one may raise is that the resulting regression function is not smooth and that it cannot easily be extrapolated.

  • A crucial question that one may have is whether this GBM only extracts the structural (systematic) part or whether it also reflects some of the noise in the observed data. The latter would not be good, because the noisy part is not structure that will repeat in the future.

  • The previous issue is known as in-sample over-fitting.

  • In-sample over-fitting is a recurrent question to all statistical and machine learning methods, and it will be discussed in detail below.
